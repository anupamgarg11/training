01

What the data actually says

Six findings, ordered by cost. Two of them come from your Apple Watch and neither is what you'd expect.

1
Critical · fix this first

Every run long enough to matter is run too hard.

Of your 28 runs with heart-rate data, 8 averaged under 155 bpm — so you do have an easy gear. But those were all 2.4–4.3 km, each with a 40-beat gap between average and max: run-walk sessions, not aerobic runs.

Filter to runs of 5 km or longer and it's absolute: all 15 sit between 155 and 173 bpm. Your "5k easy run" averaged 161. Your "7k easy run": 160. The 10k: 170. Your all-out PB: 173. Your easy long runs and your maximum effort are twelve beats apart.

With a true max of 191 (9 July) and threshold near 174, easy is 140–155 bpm. Everything you do lives in the gray zone — too hard to recover from, too easy to drive adaptation. It explains the pace plateau, the permanent leg soreness, and why leg day keeps getting skipped.

2
Critical · confirmed by your watch

You sleep 5.9 hours, and 45% of nights are under six.

You estimated six hours. Across 87 logged nights since April the mean is 5.91 h — 45% under 6 h, 68% under 7 h, only 28 nights at 7 h or more. Your watch-wearing is patchy, so treat the exact figure loosely; but the nights it did capture skew low, and unrecorded nights are more likely to be late ones, not early ones.

At 5–6 h with six training days a week you suppress muscle protein synthesis, slow glycogen replenishment, raise perceived effort at any given pace, and materially increase soft-tissue injury risk — during the exact 4× mileage ramp we're about to run.

It is almost certainly connected to something else in your data: 19 of 40 runs started after 8 pm, one at midnight. Hard running late keeps core temperature and adrenaline elevated when they need to fall. The late running costs you the sleep; the lost sleep costs you the training.

Credit where it's due — this is improving. You were at 4–5 h through late 2025 and you're at 5.8–6.4 h now. Getting to 7 h would do more for you over 17 weeks than any programming change on this page.

3
Critical

Legs aren't "sometimes skipped." They've been gone since June 5th.

In 26 weeks you had 14 sessions with meaningful leg work, and the last real one was 5 June. Since then, two token appearances — one titled "shoulder and incomplete legs." Pull-ups, standing calf raises and back extensions haven't been logged since May either.

Weekly: 0.9 sets hamstrings/glutes, 2.2 quads, against 7.9 biceps and 6.6 triceps. No hip hinge appears anywhere in 354 sessions. For a half marathon in December, your posterior chain is both your propulsion and your injury insurance, and it's the least trained thing you own.

It self-perpetuates: legs are sore because you run everything at threshold, so you skip legs, so legs stay weak, so running hurts more. Fixing run intensity is what unlocks leg day.

4
New — from your running-form data

Your running mechanics are costing you free speed.

Apple Health has been recording your form, and two numbers stand out. Ground contact time: 285 ms. Trained runners sit at 200–240 ms; above 280 is the inefficient band. Vertical oscillation: 11.5 cm. Efficient runners are 6–9 cm. You're spending too long on the ground and too much energy going up instead of forward.

Both are classic signatures of overstriding with a low cadence, on top of an underpowered posterior chain that can't return elastic energy. Which is to say: they're the mechanical fingerprint of finding 3.

The fix is two things you're already about to do — heavy calf work and strides — plus one new one: deliberately raise your cadence toward 170–175 steps per minute. Shorter, quicker steps, landing under your hips rather than out in front. Run easy sessions to a 170 bpm playlist or metronome. This is the rare change that makes you faster and reduces impact at the same time.

5
Redundancy — you asked what to cut

Roughly a third of your gym volume is duplicated work.

  • Chest — 138 fly sets against 57 press sets. Cable Fly (73) + Low Cable Fly (51) + Machine Fly (10) + Pec Deck (4). Flies are the weakest chest builder per set and you're flying 2.4× more than pressing.
  • Triceps — 138 sets of one movement. Rope Pushdown (49) + Pushdown (45) + Cable Kickback (37) + Single-Arm Pushdown (7). All elbow-extension-at-the-side. Only 33 overhead sets — and the long head, the largest part of the triceps, only grows in the stretched overhead position.
  • Biceps — 204 sets across six near-identical curls. Hammer, incline, BB preacher, DB preacher, reverse, behind-the-back.
  • Core — 156 sets, all spinal flexion. Cable crunch at 75 kg × 20, knee raise, side bend. Zero anti-rotation, zero carries. Side Bend (42 sets) works against you — loaded lateral flexion thickens the waist you're trying to narrow.
6
The real risk to December

Travel doesn't slow you down — it takes you to zero.

April–May you were flawless: six training days a week, three runs, 13–18 km, every week. Then June and July: weeks of 0, 2, 2, 0 and 3 training days. Four weeks lost, exactly when a base should have been building.

The cost is measurable. In May you ran 6:45/km at 160 bpm. By August, at that same 160 bpm, you ran 7:41/km — 55 seconds per kilometre slower at identical cardiac cost. Your resting heart rate tells the same story: 56.3 bpm in May, your best month, spiking to 62.4 in June.

Seventeen weeks contains at least one more trip. There's a fallback protocol at the bottom of this page. The rule is never zero.

Realistic targets

Your 28:48 5k projects to about 2:12 by formula — but formulas assume matched endurance, and at 10 km/week you don't have it. With the correction for your mileage, your today half marathon is roughly 2:18–2:22.

Seventeen weeks changes that. You went 33:42 → 28:48 for 5k in three months of under-fuelled, travel-disrupted training. Fed and structured, a 26:00–26:30 5k by November is realistic.

Plan for 2:10 (6:10/km). Expect 2:12–2:18 on the day — first half marathons cost a few minutes to inexperience. Sub-2:05 is live if your week-11 10k comes in under 57:00. Sub-2:00 needs it under 55:00: possible, not plannable.

Run the first 3 km at 6:20/km regardless of how good you feel. Going out at 5:45 on adrenaline is how most first half marathons die at kilometre 16.

The three rules for a two-run block

1. Protect the long-run progression above all else. Weekly volume will drop about 30% and that's acceptable. The long run stalling is not — it's the single variable that determines whether 21 km is survivable in December.

2. If two-run weeks run three or more deep, flatten the long run rather than continuing to climb. Hold it at the last distance you hit until you're back to three runs. Growing a long run on a shrinking base is how people get injured in October.

3. Don't replace the lost mileage with intensity. The temptation is to make the two runs harder to compensate. That's the exact error this entire plan exists to correct. If you want the aerobic work back, add an easy 30-minute incline walk or your pickleball game — impact-free, and it keeps the base ticking over.

Nutrition

Your Apple Watch measured TDEE at ~2,300–2,350 on well-worn days, confirming your instinct that 2,400 is roughly maintenance. You picked 1,900. That holds — but the number has to rise with your mileage to stay the same deficit.

Correction: your weekends aren't the problem

I assumed a ~3,100 kcal weekend and built an argument on it. Your actual Sunday — one larger meal around 1,200, one standard meal at 500–600, some incidental extra — lands nearer 2,000–2,300. That's a normal day, not a blowout. Your weekly average is roughly 1,950, which is a genuine and reasonably disciplined ~375 kcal deficit. The restrict-rebound theory doesn't apply to you; scratch it.

Which means your eight stalled lifts have a simpler explanation: eight months of training in a deficit, at 16–20 reps, with no load progression. In a deficit you don't gain much regardless — but you should at least hold and slowly add strength, and high-rep work at a fixed weight won't even do that.

You chose 1,900. Here's the version of that which won't break in November.

1,900 against today's maintenance of ~2,325 is a 425 kcal deficit — about 18%. That's a defensible, well-chosen number, and for the next eight weeks it's genuinely fine.

The trap is what happens if you hold it. As mileage triples, your maintenance climbs to roughly 2,620 by week 12. Hold 1,900 through that and your deficit silently widens from 425 to 720 kcal — you'd be nearly doubling the aggression at the exact moment the training gets hardest, without ever deciding to. Energy availability would fall to ~24 kcal/kg lean mass, which is where adaptation stops and injuries start.

So the table below keeps your deficit fixed at ~425 and lets the plate follow the workload. You are not eating more; you're holding the same deficit you picked. That produces roughly 0.4–0.45 kg/week — about 6–7 kg across 17 weeks, which takes you from ~71 kg at 20% to around 64–65 kg at roughly 13%. That is a visible-abs physique, arrived at trained rather than depleted.

Three tripwires — if any fires, add 250 kcal of carbs

A 425 kcal deficit alongside a 4× mileage ramp is at the aggressive end for holding muscle. That's a legitimate choice, but it needs objective abort criteria rather than feel, because "I'm fine" is exactly what under-recovery says.

1. Your week-11 10k time trial is slower than your week-5 5k pace predicts. 2. Resting heart rate climbs 4+ bpm above your rolling baseline for more than a week. 3. Weight drops faster than 0.6 kg/week for two consecutive weeks.

Any one of those means the deficit has outrun the training. Add the calories as carbohydrate, keep protein where it is, and reassess in two weeks. You'll still get lean — a fortnight at a smaller deficit costs almost nothing, and a stress fracture in November costs the race.

The four changes that get you there

  • Eat breakfast. Biggest single lever — you're skipping ~475 kcal and 25 g protein, then wondering why the evening is hard.
  • Chicken 200 g → 300 g/day. 150 g per meal instead of 100 g. Ask your cook to weigh it raw; it's the one thing worth measuring.
  • Double the vegetables. Broccoli, beans, zucchini, spinach. You're near 12–15 g fibre; target 30 g. You already spotted this.
  • Greek yogurt 50 g → 150 g. Cheap protein and live cultures for the gut goal you mentioned.

Tracking, supplements, water

  • Don't log everything — you'll quit. Weigh the raw chicken, and weigh yourself 3–4 mornings a week. The two-week weight trend beats any calorie app because it measures reality, not your estimate of it. Losing faster than 0.4 kg/week? Eat more.
  • Water 2 L → 3–3.5 L. Bengaluru, evening running, and you're already short.
  • Creatine 5 g, omega-3, prebiotic, magnesium, zinc — all good, keep as is.
  • Check your vitamin D dose. Weekly is fine at 60,000 IU (standard in India); 2,000 IU weekly is not enough.
  • Get ferritin, vitamin D and B12 tested before week 6. Four months of new running plus a deficit is exactly where low ferritin shows up — and it presents as "my easy runs feel awful."

The travel protocol

The single most likely thing to cost you December. June and July already showed you what happens without one.

Minimum viable week — never go below this

  • 2 runs. One easy 5 km, one as long as the plan allows. Hotel treadmill counts.
  • 2 full-body sessions, 30 minutes, bodyweight and band.
  • Four sessions holds roughly 80% of your fitness on 40% of the time.
  • Pack a resistance band. Weighs nothing, removes every excuse.

The 30-minute hotel session

  • Bulgarian split squat — 3 × 12/leg
  • Single-leg RDL — 3 × 10/leg
  • Feet-elevated push-ups — 3 × max
  • Band row — 3 × 15
  • Band Pallof press — 3 × 10/side
  • Calf raise off a step — 3 × 20
  • Plank — 3 × 45 s

If a week gets lost anyway

Don't catch up. Repeat the previous week's volume once, then rejoin where the calendar says. Cramming two weeks into one is how you arrive in December injured instead of undertrained — and undertrained still finishes the race.

Why it has to be three phases and not one

You cannot cut your way to more muscle. The legs, lats and delts you're missing require a period of eating at or slightly above maintenance with heavy lifting as the priority — which is the exact opposite of what a half-marathon build in a deficit demands. Trying to do both at once is how people spend three years going nowhere.

So: take the fat off and build a real aerobic engine by December. Then flip — four lifting days, running dropped to two maintenance sessions a week, a small surplus, and eight months of actually driving the lifts that have sat still since January. Then take the fat back off in late 2027.

The number to notice is the end state: ~67 kg at 10%, against 71 kg at 20% today. Four kilos lighter, carrying roughly four kilos more muscle and six kilos less fat. That's a transformation, and it lands you very close to the second and third photos.

The one thing that would derail it

Natural muscle gain at your training age is roughly 2–4 kg a year under good conditions — and that's the rate limiter on this entire timeline, not effort or programming. It cannot be rushed, and it goes to near zero in a deficit.

Which means the highest-risk decision in front of you isn't this plan. It's what you do in January. The instinct after seeing abs in December will be to keep cutting to chase the last few percent. Don't. That's the point where you'd trade the physique you actually want for a leaner version of the one you already have.
